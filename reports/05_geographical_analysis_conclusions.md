# 05. Geographical Analysis — Conclusions

## Purpose

This analysis examines the geographical distribution of reported seizure records in the UNODC Individual Drug Seizures (IDS) dataset. It focuses on country-level reporting concentration, country drug-class profiles, regional and subregional patterns, and the extent to which regional profiles depend on their largest contributing country.

The analysis covers **2011–2025**. The 2026 observations are partial and are not included in the main comparisons.

## Main findings

### 1. Geographical reporting is highly concentrated

Several subregions are strongly dominated by one country's contribution to the dataset:

- **South America:** Colombia is the principal contributor.
- **Eastern Europe:** Russia accounts for a large share of reported records.
- **East and South-East Asia:** the Philippines is a major contributor.
- **West and Central Africa:** Nigeria is a major contributor.

This concentration means that an apparent regional or subregional pattern may partly reflect the reporting profile of one country rather than a pattern shared evenly across all countries in that area.

### 2. Some subregional drug-class profiles are relatively robust

The robustness analysis indicates that some subregional profiles remain broadly similar after excluding the largest contributing country. This applies to:

- South America
- Central America
- South Asia
- Central Asia and Transcaucasia
- Western and Central Europe

For these subregions, the broad drug-class composition is not explained entirely by the largest reporting country.

### 3. Other subregional profiles are strongly country-driven

In other subregions, the prominence of particular drug classes changes substantially after excluding the leading country. Examples include:

- NPS in East Africa
- Amphetamines in East and South-East Asia
- Opioids in North America
- Sedatives in West and Central Africa

These patterns should therefore be interpreted cautiously: the apparent subregional profile may be heavily influenced by one country's reporting contribution.

### 4. Regional aggregation can conceal important differences

Two subregions may show similar overall drug-class profiles while having very different country-level contributions. Looking only at broad regional totals can therefore hide meaningful variation in the countries that make up those totals.

The country and subregion analyses should be read together rather than treating regional aggregates as homogeneous.

### 5. These findings describe seizure reporting, not drug prevalence or consumption

The results concern the distribution and composition of records reported to the IDS dataset. Differences across countries and regions can reflect national enforcement priorities, reporting practices, data coverage, and drug-classification procedures.

The number of records should not be interpreted as a direct measure of drug prevalence, consumption, market size, seizure quantity, or trafficking flows. The dataset does not provide origin–destination country pairs from which trafficking routes could be inferred.

## Interpretation and limitations

- **Reporting concentration:** A high number or share of records from one country can strongly influence the profile of its subregion.
- **Uneven coverage:** Countries differ in the number of years and records they contribute. A country's absence from a particular year does not necessarily mean that no seizures occurred.
- **Record counts:** The unit of analysis is the original source record. Counts do not measure the amount of drugs seized; quantities also cannot be aggregated across different measurement units.
- **Drug classification:** Records that do not match the drug reference classification are retained as `Unclassified`. Their presence should be considered when interpreting class shares.
- **Robustness check:** Excluding the largest reporting country is a descriptive sensitivity analysis. It helps assess dependence on that country but does not make the remaining countries representative of the subregion.
- **Time coverage:** 2026 is partial and should not be compared directly with complete years.

## Overall conclusion

The geographical analysis shows that IDS seizure reporting is highly concentrated in a number of countries and that this concentration shapes some regional and subregional drug-class profiles. Some profiles remain broadly similar after excluding their largest contributor, while others change considerably. This demonstrates why country-level contributions and robustness checks are essential when interpreting regional aggregates.

The findings should be understood as patterns in reported seizure records, shaped by both seizure activity and the processes through which countries report data, rather than as direct evidence of the geographical distribution of drug markets or consumption.
